Report of the Referee -- DT18474/Kim
----------------------------------------------------------------------

This is an interesting paper that can contribute to the phenomenology of the Planck-scale, in particular looking for blurring effects due to the fundamental uncertainty of temporal/spatial measurements; there's a long history of attempting to observe this in AGNs and GRBs in both time-delay (i.e. Lorentz invariance) and spatial blurring; the latter already modeled as an incremental-scattering exponent "alpha". The question is, of course, how strong this can be (i.e. a smallest-possible alpha) and agree with observations, and not be attributable to instrumental effects; there is strong physical motivation to expect a value of 2/3, as it is consistent with holographic quantum-gravity and well-known aspects of black-hole physics. The authors report on related calculations regarding line-width broadening expected for GRB and CMB observations, comparing these with already-established limits from spatial-broadening measurements - in particular for GRB221009A.

The authors follow an approach of considering an arrival-time jitter, and then use this to estimate fractions of photons lost to the mean (core) of the line; it can be compared to the path-length approach used elsewhere, and so is related to previous limits on alpha, with caveats. I don't have serious reservations to their calculations - given clear descriptions of their limitations - and trust the numbers reported, as they follow a reasonable and well-described logic of scattering OoM estimates, and whether those photons not found within the core of the instrumental PSF are retained in some "pedestal". In optical photometry, this is typically refered to as a "halo." Mentioning this previous nomenclature adopted by others may help show how their work compares. The observational reason for this is that both AGNs and GRBs have to be viewed (depending on wavelength) as potentially extended sources; that should be mentioned here, although it is understood that this result deals with line-broadening, not spatial blurring or localizability; although both effects effectively scatter photons out of the telescope beam. Extending this analysis to CMB photons gives a weaker constraint, but there is still value in attempting it, as that seems consistent with the previous work on AGNs and GRBs.

The main problem with authors' approach is that it does not sufficiently address how real telescopes actually sense photons, and therefore the accumulated effect of any spatial or spectral dispersion due to foam; not merely scattering to the horizon (or out of band) any that don't fall within the resolution limits of their beams, as assumed here. The authors can more clearly state that assumption at the outset; it is dealt with correctly in Steinbring, 2023 [reference 12], but here is only treated as "flat" or uniform scattering outside the intrinsic instrumental PSF assuming a single, monochromatic strength of blurring, as far as I can tell. It does not report an observed averaged effect over the bandpass, as a real telescope would. But, that said, a healthy approach for this field is to consider different ways of calculating this scattering effect, and so whether those approaches at least give consistent results. It seems they find, in this way, that the GeV/TeV linewidths of GRB221009A are too broad to allow for a simple random-walk (alpha=0.5) but agree with alpha=0.65; so that is consistent with previous results, and a useful result. But that result does not preclude the *range* of values which [12] finds as consistent with observations: 0.650 ≤ alpha ≤ 0.735. As those results have a best-fit value near 2/3 (the case consistent with holographic quantum-foam) they are not merely a limit, and furthermore, are consistent with that model PSF for all observations of GRB221009A (and all other GRBs observed) from TeV through to X-rays, UV and through to the optical - and previous results with AGNs. It is an important distinction: that agreement would not be true, if alpha was either higher *or* lower than that range. So, those results are, in effect, a first *detection*, effectively dismissed here as merely not having sensed foam as strong as alpha of 0.65 by those authors' interpretation - which is incorrect.

That said, there seems to me two significant failings in their connection of this to the existing literature on the subject; possibly as it's a much smaller community than that studying Lorentz invariance, and less well cited. These problems can be easily remedied in two ways, I think:

First is that, although they credit Ng-Perlman as the originators of the spatial blurring approach using exponent alpha (based on their 2022 review paper in Universe), this does not reflect the more sophisticated treatment of spatial blurring incorporating telescope optics, which is the later work of Steinbring and Ng (following the Steinbring, 2015, ApJ, 802, 38 approach; here uncited). This also corrects a flaw of not accounting for more-blurred, bluer photons reported in Steinbring, 2007, ApJ, 655, 714 (also uncited). That revised approach demands that any scattered photon, including those redshifted bluer ones, cannot contribute when falling outside the FoV of the telescope; assuming that this is full stochastic process that is the average of any less-scattered photons. The important effect is to allow a *larger* observed alpha; this resolves the issue of Ng and Perlman, which cannot be correct, as that gives a *single* value of angular phase dispersion (delta) and would result (unless alpha is larger) in all photons at TeV energies being scattered over the whole sky (the Fermi LAT PSF would be larger than its FoV): so no GRBs should be localizable. That incorrect interpretation is what the authors compare to in the abstract, and elsewhere. So, to be correct, this should properly be referred to as the Ng-Steinbring relation, I believe, as Ng-Perlman would have been ruled out by GRB observations. Acknowledgement of that, and that the Steinbring (2023) result would then be a first detection of foam-induced spatial blurring - implying that the in-orbit observed Fermi LAT PSF is, in fact, broadened by the effects of holographic quantum foam - seems to fairly place those results in context. Can the authors estimate, if incorporating the Ng-Steinbring approach to alpha on the Fermi LAT data, that they too find a value of alpha>0.65 is favoured?

Second is that the other part of the history of this field missing from their introduction is, of course, that linewidth-jitter blurring by foam has already been looked for in the optical by Cooke et al., 2020, MNRAS, 494, 4 (available at: https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/staa440) using a similar calcuation. My view is that there should be some discussion of how this current work compares to that, and it should be cited here.

Also: the reference to [13] E. Steinbring and Y. J. Ng, arXiv e-prints 10.48550/arXiv.2603.18277 (2026), arXiv:2603.18277, is now published. It should be, I think: E. Steinbring and Y. J. Ng, 2026, Proceedings of Science, Proceedings of the Corfu Summer Institute 2025 "School and Workshops on Elementary Particle Physics and Gravity" (CORFU2025) 205 [2026eppg.confE.205S], depending on the bibliographic style PRD uses.

So overall, I think the paper can make a good contribution to this discussion, but does *not* move (although is consistent with) the currently-known limits of the alpha-value: a holographic value of 2/3 is still favoured; an alpha=0.65 was already ruled out by images of quasars with Hubble more than 20 years ago. In my view, with the recommended corrections, and thus indicating missing connections to extant work in the field, and so the already established limits to alpha (given that exponent's correctly-revised interpretation) the paper can be acceptable for publication in PRD by adding another approach to putting limits on foam-induced blurring.
